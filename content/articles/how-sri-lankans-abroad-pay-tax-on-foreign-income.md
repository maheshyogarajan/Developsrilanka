If you are a Sri Lankan earning money outside the country — whether you're a freelancer with overseas clients, a remote employee of a foreign company, an investor with offshore holdings, or a returning migrant with savings — Sri Lanka's tax law has a clear position on what you owe. This guide walks you through it, with the section numbers so you can verify everything in the **Inland Revenue Act No. 24 of 2017** (as amended through 31 March 2025).

## The two questions that decide everything

Two questions decide whether you owe Sri Lankan tax on your foreign income:

1. **Are you a Sri Lankan tax resident for the year of assessment?**
2. **Is the income foreign-sourced, and has any of it been remitted?**

Answering both gets you 90% of the way to the right tax position. Let's take them in order.

## Are you a Sri Lankan resident?

The residency test lives in **§69 of the IRA**. You are a Sri Lankan tax resident for a year of assessment if **any** of these is true:

- **(a) You reside in Sri Lanka.** This is a facts-and-circumstances test — a permanent home, family ties, the centre of your economic life.
- **(b) You are physically present in Sri Lanka for 183 days or more** in any twelve-month period that commences or ends during the year of assessment. This is the bright-line test most people use. Count days carefully — arrival and departure days both count, regardless of the hour.
- **(c) You are a Sri Lankan government employee or official posted abroad and your spouse is also posted abroad** during the year.
- **(d) You are employed on a Sri Lankan ship** within the meaning of the Merchant Shipping Act, for the period of that employment.

If none of these applies, you are a **non-resident** for that year. Non-residents are taxed only on Sri Lankan-sourced income — your foreign-sourced income is outside the Sri Lankan tax net entirely.

If even one applies, you are a **resident**. Resident individuals have a worldwide tax base, with foreign-source income brought in on the remittance basis (more on that below).

## What counts as "foreign-source"?

The source rules are in **§71 of the IRA**. The principle is:

> A person's income from an employment, business, investment, or other source is treated as having a Sri Lankan source to the extent the activity, payment, or asset is connected to Sri Lanka. Everything else is foreign-source.

For practical purposes:

- **Employment income** is sourced where the work is physically performed. If you sit in Colombo writing code for a London company, your salary is Sri Lankan-source. If you sit in London writing code for a London company, it's UK-source.
- **Business income** is sourced where the business is carried on. A consulting practice with its clients, contracts, and operations in Sri Lanka is Sri Lankan-source — even if some clients pay in USD.
- **Investment income** (interest, dividends, rent, royalties) is sourced where the payer carries on business or the property is located. Interest from a US bond is US-source. Rent from a London flat is UK-source. Dividends from a Colombo Stock Exchange-listed company are Sri Lankan-source.
- **Capital gains** follow the location of the asset.

A common confusion: where the **bank account** is located does not determine source. A US tech company paying salary into your Sri Lankan account for work performed in Colombo is Sri Lankan-source. The same company paying you for work performed in San Francisco is US-source — whether the money lands in California or Colombo.

## The remittance basis for residents

This is where Sri Lanka diverges from most "worldwide income" tax systems. **For resident individuals, foreign-source income is assessed in practice when it is remitted to Sri Lanka** — that is, when it enters the Sri Lankan financial system.

What this means operationally:

- USD earned and left in an offshore account is not (yet) part of your Sri Lankan assessable income.
- The moment that money is transferred to a Sri Lankan bank — by SWIFT, by remittance service, by ATM withdrawal in LKR, by Sri Lankan card spend funded from a foreign card linked to an offshore account — the remitted portion becomes assessable.
- The IRD relies on Sri Lankan bank statements as the primary evidence trail. Keep them clean and tagged to source.

Practically, this is why we ask FIESTA users to upload their Sri Lankan bank statements: those are the legally relevant record of remittance, and they're what the IRD will ask to see in an audit.

## How is it taxed once it lands?

Sri Lankan personal income tax for the **2025/26 year of assessment** uses a dual-track structure:

| Band | Local-source rate | Foreign-source rate |
|---|---|---|
| First Rs 1,800,000 personal relief | 0% | 0% |
| Next Rs 1,000,000 of taxable income | 6% | 6% |
| Above that, **foreign-source** | — | **15% flat (cap)** |
| Rs 1M – Rs 1.5M (above first band) | 18% | — |
| Rs 1.5M – Rs 2M | 24% | — |
| Rs 2M – Rs 2.5M | 30% | — |
| Above Rs 2.5M | 36% | — |

The 15% flat cap on foreign-source income above the first taxable band is meaningful: a high earner with all-foreign income tops out at an effective rate close to 15%, while the equivalent local-source earner faces marginal rates up to 36%.

(See **First Schedule, Part I of the IRA**, as amended. Rates change by gazette; FIESTA pins them in `slabs.yaml` and updates on gazette publication.)

The personal relief of Rs 1,800,000 applies regardless of source. Senior citizens (age 60+) get an additional Rs 500,000 relief under **§51**.

## Avoiding double tax — the foreign tax credit

If you've already paid tax abroad on the same income, **§80 of the IRA** lets you claim a credit for that foreign tax against your Sri Lankan liability. This prevents the same dollar being taxed twice.

The mechanics (under **§81**):

- The credit is calculated **separately for each source** — each employment, each business, each investment — and separately for each capital-gain realisation.
- The credit **cannot exceed your average Sri Lankan tax rate** applied to that foreign-source income. (You can't use US tax on US income to wipe out Sri Lankan tax on Sri Lankan income.)
- The foreign tax must be **paid within two years** of the end of the year in which the foreign-source income was derived.
- Unused credit cannot be refunded, carried back, or carried forward.

The proof is a foreign tax certificate — a W-2 in the US, a P60 in the UK, a payslip showing year-to-date tax withheld in jurisdictions without an annual statement, or the equivalent. Keep these. They're what unlocks the credit.

Sri Lanka's network of **Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAAs)** sits on top of §80. Where a DTAA applies, the treaty rules can:

- Reallocate the taxing right (e.g. employment income earned in a country for fewer than 183 days may be taxable in the country of residence only),
- Reduce the rate of withholding tax at source (e.g. capping treaty withholding on dividends at 10% rather than the statute's higher domestic rate),
- Provide a specific credit mechanism that overrides §80.

The DTAA never *increases* tax. It only reduces or reallocates. We have separate guides per major DTAA partner — US, UK, Australia — coming in subsequent articles.

## What you need to file

If you are a Sri Lankan resident with foreign-source income that has been remitted, you must file a **Return of Income (Form IT/IT/02)** by **30 November** following the end of the year of assessment.

For 2025/26 (1 April 2025 – 31 March 2026), the return is due by **30 November 2026**.

Tax shown as payable on assessment is due the same date. If you are an instalment payer under **§90** (you have business or investment income, or employment income from a non-withholding employer), the instalments are due on:

- **15 August** (first quarter)
- **15 November** (second quarter)
- **15 February** (third quarter)
- **15 May** (fourth quarter, paid in the following year of assessment)

What you'll need to assemble:

1. **Sri Lankan bank statements** for the full year — these are the remittance record.
2. **Foreign payslips / contracts / 1099s / payment records** showing the gross foreign income, source, and any tax withheld abroad.
3. **Foreign tax certificates** to back any §80 credit claim.
4. **A reconciliation** between gross foreign income (in original currency) and remitted amounts (in LKR at applied exchange rates).
5. **Records of any deductions** you intend to claim (Sri Lankan-source business expenses if relevant, qualifying payments under §52).

## A worked example

A Sri Lankan resident in Colombo, age 38, works remotely for a US tech company.

- Annual salary: USD 60,000 (gross).
- USD 50,000 remitted to a Sri Lankan bank during YA 2025/26; USD 10,000 left in a US account for college fees.
- US federal income tax withheld on the remitted portion: USD 5,000 equivalent.
- No other income.

**Step 1 — Assessable foreign income (remitted only):**
USD 50,000 at a blended LKR/USD rate of, say, 302 = LKR 15,100,000.

**Step 2 — Apply personal relief (§51):**
15,100,000 − 1,800,000 = 13,300,000 taxable.

**Step 3 — Apply dual-track 25/26 rates:**
- First Rs 1,000,000 at 6% = Rs 60,000
- Remaining Rs 12,300,000, foreign-source, at the 15% cap = Rs 1,845,000
- Total Sri Lankan tax before credit = Rs 1,905,000

**Step 4 — Foreign tax credit (§80):**
USD 5,000 at LKR 302 = LKR 1,510,000. This is below the cap (the §81 ceiling = average Sri Lankan rate on the foreign income), so the full credit is allowed.

**Net Sri Lankan tax payable: Rs 1,905,000 − Rs 1,510,000 = Rs 395,000.**

## Common mistakes that trigger trouble

- **Counting foreign-account balances as untaxed forever.** If you remit later, the remittance brings them into the net then — even years after the original earning. The IRA imposes no statute of limitations on tracking remittances.
- **Forgetting Sri Lankan-source income.** A "freelance for foreign clients" framing can hide local-source work if the work is actually done in Sri Lanka. The location of the client doesn't move the source. (See §71 again.)
- **Claiming foreign tax credit without certificates.** §81 requires proof. Lost W-2s mean lost credit.
- **Missing the 30 November deadline.** Late filing penalties under the IRA accumulate; the IRD does send notices. File on time, even if the calculation isn't perfect — you can amend.
- **Confusing residency with citizenship.** Sri Lankan citizenship has no bearing on tax residency. A non-citizen who spends 200 days in Colombo is resident; a citizen who spends 30 days is not.

## The summary

- Sri Lankan tax residency is decided by **§69** — chiefly the 183-day test.
- Residents are taxed on worldwide income; non-residents on Sri Lankan-source only (**§71**).
- Foreign-source income for residents is assessed on the **remittance basis** in practice — track what lands in your Sri Lankan bank.
- The **2025/26 dual-track** rates cap foreign-source income at **15%** above the first taxable band.
- The **foreign tax credit under §80** stops you being taxed twice on the same dollar — keep the certificates.
- The return is due **30 November** following the year of assessment.
